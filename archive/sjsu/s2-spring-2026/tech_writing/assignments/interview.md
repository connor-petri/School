# 100W Interview

### Who Where What
Don Woodward - Principle Engineer Roku
Architect of LTOS - embedded RTOS

### Inspiration
When 11 years, family visited friends. Had son his age that he hadnt met. Showed the TRS80 model 1. First computer ever used. 
10 print "don is cool"
20 goto 10

Sense of power, loves programming.

Started going to radio shack. Taught basic. Lucky: right place right time. Loved programming, good at it, and in high demand. Pay was good

### Career path
Started at Xerox. On-campus interviewing @UCSD. Made publishing software in SD. Liked word processing as a tech back then. Interviewed with Qualcomm and Xerox, but Qualcomm was still tiny and only had 1 product: and email client for Windows. The workstation software was most interesting of the two at the time. XGML editor, grandfather of HTML. US Gov required all paperwork be submitted in XGML, so the demand for this software was high. 

Went into international sales for 2 years to try to climb the corpo ladder. Not for him. 

Got an offer to write an HTML editor for another company. First commercial C++ application and first windows application. First software he was in charge of designing. That product is now Adobe Dreamweaver.

Promoted to principle engineer of dreamweaver after apple deal.

Was into multimedia, iBand is where he met CEO of Roku. Internet bubble burst which almost bankrupted the company. Aquired but not managed well, so ReplayTV went under.

Joined Roku in 2003 as CTO, left for family reasons, returned to Adobe, made own startup (video editor for the TV). Rejoined Roku in 2017

### Day in the life
In charge of the software he writes - Principle archetect. Doesn't like to be a people manager. Figure out what they need to build, figure out how to build it, and assign other engineers to the tasks. 

### What do you like best
Writing software. More enjoyable than public speaking or people managing. There is nothing like creating and shipping products. Seeing your software on a shelf is rewarding.

### What challenges do you face
Keep people true to the architecture. Everyone has their own ideas/wants to leave their mark. This causes inconsistant architecture. Stubborn engineers/short sighted engineers. People in general. As companies grow, the number of people and varying attitudes and skill sets. Dealing with this is a challenge. Feels weighed down, but that is part of the job.

Process is frustrating, as they dont need it themselves. Writing specs and test plans is frustrating.

### Most rewarding projects
- Dreamweaver - not sure if they could pull it off. Learning as they went.
- ReplayTV - magical box that could pause live TV was inventing the future
- Early Roku - Lead architect role was rewarding. True startup environment where everyone was friends. Worked in downtown Palo Alto.
- Adobe - Putting flash player on TVs back when everyone wanted it. Had connections. Did a good job/proved himself.
- LTOS - Not many companies have the ability to develop an OS and be successful. Doing that is rewarding. LTOS is small, fast, and allows for developing platform-independent C code. Very satisfied with LT and how it worked out.
- Most rewarding: Adobe, lead a team of 5. Gave them a difficult problem to solve while architecting the project. They needed hardware acceleration for flash on a TV. At one point the manager said they solved the problem. Arranged for a member of this team to transfer to SF to get her out of a mysoginistic work environment. As a result, she flourished and now basically runs youtube.
- Many artists saw the internet take away their jobs, many thanked him for creating dreamweaver because they could make art on a computer.
- Improving people's lives is more important and rewarding than the technical stuff

### Career advice
- Have a high standard for yourself. Dont accept mediocrety. Dont have bugs in code. 
- Understand what your code is doing. Debug via understanding not by trial and error
- Timing, luck, and networking. Internships are good.
- Honesty: know what you don't know. Don't let your ego get in the way. Own up to your mistakes.
- You cannot dislike working with someone for personal reasons. That is unprofessional. Look at yourself and why this is the case.
- Asking why is good to a point, but challenging the why can be unprofessional.
- Be open to new ideas.
- Be aware of the way you go about your job and how it affects other people.
